Saison Experiment

06/03/2012Beer#9, beer, homebrew, saisonescowles

We have long wanted to brew our own saison. Â After all, it’s the style of beer that really opened my eyes to the possibilities of beer and got us more interested in brewing and sampling as much as we could. Â But it was only recently when I started reading about saison in the Oxford Companion to Beer that I really understood just how complicated it is.

First of all, while there is a general style of base beer that is the most popular for making saison, you can in fact make sour beer from any base beer. Â And then there are several different species of Brettanomyces yeast, each with its own character and intensity. Â There are also a couple different kinds of bacteria that sour beer. Â And of course, there are a bunch of variables in the souring process, such as when you add Brett./bacteria (primary fermentation, secondary, bottling), or how warm you ferment (Dupont is supposed to ferment at 90°, for example).

So, we found a Saison Dupont clone recipe online that looked about right, and picked up five different vials of yeast: a straight saison yeast, three different Brett. yeasts (B. bruxellensis, B. claussenii, and B. lambicus), and Belgian Sour Mix (with Brett. and bacteria).

This was our second all-grain brew, and it went pretty smoothly. Â Our 5-gallon water cooler mash tun didn’t hold temperature, but we kept adding hot water until it was warm enough. Â The mash and boil went great, and after adding water to make up five gallons, the gravity was exactly on target (1.057). Â We also used fine mesh hop sacks this time instead of cheesecloth, which made for much less gunk to filter out of the wort.

After the boil, we put the wort into our bottling bucket and filled five 1-gallon growlers, one with the Belgian Sour Mix, the second with just regular saison yeast, and the last three with saison yeast and one kind of Brett. Â We’re hoping this will help us figure out what kind of saison we want to brew a larger batch of later this summer. Â And if everything works out, this should be a great way to try out a bunch of different options in small batches. Â There are so many options, especially for mead and cider, and it would be nice to have a good process to try out a lot of options and see what we like.

Trappist breweries are irritated about US “Trappist” brews

05/16/2012Beerbeer, belgium, trappistescowles

Apparently the Trappists are irritated that there are a lot of American beers with the word “Trappist” in their names:

The fact that a number of breweries in the USA are using the word Trappist to describe beers that are clearly not produced by Trappist monks has drawn the attention of the International Trappist Association, or I.T.A. The Association considers it a growing problem. For one thing, they don’t like the idea that a consumer might mistake a commercially brewed American beer for an Authentic Trappist brew.

Though I have some sympathy for their frustration, I think the bigger problem is that Americans don’t know what “Trappist” means. Â Forget getting confused about whether a beer is made by Trappist monks or not, before I started researching Belgian beer for my trip I didn’t know that there were monks making beer for public consumption, much less associate that with “Trappist”.

As the weather turns a little warmer, our thoughts are turning to lighter beers. Â We’re also planning on making a trip to Germany in a few weeks, so naturally we’ve been drinking Bavarian hefeweizen, aka weissbier. Â As it turns out, we’re going to Cologne not Munich. Â But we can’t find much Kolsch in shops (other than the very nice Früh), so we’ll just have to work on that when we get there. Â Weisse, on the other hand, is available everywhere, from the specialty beer shops to the grocery store, and even on the train.

Our favorite is probably theÂ Paulaner Hefe-WeissbierÂ — it has a little more character than your average hef, fruity and even a little sour. Â TheirÂ DunkelÂ is also very good, nutty and a little dry.

We also quite likedÂ Weihenstephaner VitusÂ (a stronger weizenbock), which was malty and creamy, while retaining the usual grassy weisse notes, with just a hint of herbs to round it out.

For a straightforward, no-nonsense weisse, my favorite is definitelyÂ Löwenbräu LöwenWeisse. Â It’sÂ sweet and grassy, light and refreshing, and I can imagine happily drinking it by the liter in a tent somewhere.

Of the other weissbiers we’ve had recently, the most striking thing about them is their consistency. We’ve been drinking mostly Belgian beer lately, and it’s easy to forget what a well-defined style is like, with beer after beer hitting all the standard notes, with only small differences. This means they’re all pretty good. On the downside, however, there’s much less excitement with these beers – very little chance of getting a surprisingly Â outstanding beer. Wind has taken to calling weissbeir the “merlot of beers” – never terrible, but never fantastic, either

I’ve also been reading through the Oxford Companion to Beer, which talks about Germany’s very traditional brewery apprenticeship program and speculates that this might be why German breweries tend to stick with traditional styles and fine-tune their recipes, rather than experiment more widely (or wildly) like the Americans, or even the Belgians.

In any event, the rest of the lot are all perfectly good. Â Based on our experience, I’d trust pretty much any German wheat beer to be good and conform to style.

  • Spaten Franziskaner Hefe-Weissbier: malty, light and creamy.
  • Schofferhofer Weizen
  • Erdinger Dunkel: rich smoky & grassy. Â Not as different from standard weisse as I had expected dunkel to be.
  • Hacker-Pschorr Hefe Weisse: grainy and a bit astringent, but still solid.
  • Schneider Weisse Tap 7: highly carbonated with a nice bit, but otherwise mild and grassy.
  • Andechs Weissbier: moderate carbonation and a little astringency provide a good bite
  • Grolsch Weizen: we had to have one Dutch weizen on the list, which we picked up at our local grocery store. Â Holds its own with the Germans just fine.
