My First Zwanze Day

09/24/2014Beerbelgium, cantillon, orlando, redlight redlight, zwanzeday, zwanzeday2014escowles

We were down in Orlando this weekend to visit the new Harry Potter Diagon Alley part of the Universal Studios theme park with the kids.Â  I snuck off for a couple of hours to head up to Redlight Redlight for my first Zwanze Day (Cantillon’s annual release where about 50 bars around the world get a single keg of a special beer brewed for the occasion).

I dithered a bit about whether it was worth the hassle.Â  I first heard about Zwanze day a couple years ago, and Redlight Redlight has been the only site in Florida.Â  It’s on the other side of town from where we usually go for the theme parks, so I always balked the logistics of driving there and having to sober up before heading back, or the price of taking a taxi back and forth across Orlando.Â  But this year I decided it was worth the hassle, and I tried Uber for the first time for the trip there (which worked out much better than a taxi, and was a lot cheaper).Â  In fact, Uber went so well that it took me about 35 minutes to get from the middle of Universal Studios to Redlight Redlight, instead of the hour-plus I expected.

When I got there about 2½ hours before opening time, I was a little surprised that there was no line yet.Â  Cantillon is one of the most-loved-by-beer-geeks breweries in the world, and the annual Zwanze release is their most sought-after.Â  So I killed a little time walking around the neighborhood and started standing in line when a few other people showed up.Â  I’m lucky that I was at the front of the line, because the tickets for the Zwanze beer itself were sold out before I decided I would go, and I got a ticket only because there had been a cancellation.

The wait went quickly since I was chatting with a group of guys from Ocala, and we quickly grabbed a table when the event started and proceeded to share glasses of almost everything on the impressive taplist.Â  Some highlights:

  • Zwanze 2014 (Cuvée Florian): of course this was the main event, and lived up to my expectations.Â  This year’s beer was a cross between Iris and a kriek.Â  Iris is much hoppier than typical Belgian beers, but the hops were very gentle here, and the cherries were also toned down.Â  The result was a delightful sour beer, with just a beautiful ruby color, and a little earthy cherry flavor.
  • Redlight Redlight also had Cantillon’s Iris, Gueuze, and Mamouche on tap.Â  I’d had the Gueuze several times when we lived in the Netherlands, and it was fantastic as always.Â  Iris and Mamouche were both new to me: Iris I liked immediately and especially since it’s one part of the Zwanze blend, it was nice to compare them side-by-side.Â  Mamouche at first seemed too herbal and bitter to me, but it grew on me as I sipped at.
  • I was disappointed this summer that I didn’t get up to Hill Farmstead in Vermont.Â  So I was doubly glad to have their collaboration with Blaugies, La Vermontoise.Â  I had somehow gotten it into my head that this was a sour, so I was surprised when it turned out to be a saison, and a mild and smooth one at that.Â  But once I adjusted to that, I found this to be a very nice, sweet, floral beer.Â  So now I’m really irritated that I didn’t get up there to get a case of it…
  • Angry Chair’s 3 Little Birds was a very nice Berliner Weisse, with a very subtle tropical fruit character.Â  It especially benefited from a direct comparison to Cigar City’s Passion Guava Grove, which is a much bolder beer that seemed overbearing in contrast.
  • The only stout we tried was Mikkeller’s Beer Geek Brunch Weasel, which was fantastic, and the perfect contrast to the saisons and sours.
  • I finished out the afternoon with The Bruery’s Oude Tart.Â  This is a wonderful sour I’ve had several times before and always enjoyed.Â  It has more body and sweetness than the Cantillon sours, and felt like a perfectly-balanced way to end.

Not only did I have some wonderful beers, I also met some nice people and had a great time sharing and talking about the beers with them.Â  This really ties into a few posts (e.g., this one from Boak & Bailey) that I’ve seen in the last few days about the marketing strategy for Shock Top, which boils down to positioning it as a simpler, more approachable craft beer to people who think Shock Top is made by a small/independent brewery (instead of AB-InBev).Â  I guess I see the parallel with overcomplicated, mystique-shrouded wine snobbery, but beer is a lot cheaper, and I’ve consistently seen bartenders hand out a bunch of samples and do their best to find the best beer for anyone who walks in the door.

Trappist breweries are irritated about US “Trappist” brews

05/16/2012Beerbeer, belgium, trappistescowles

Apparently the Trappists are irritated that there are a lot of American beers with the word “Trappist” in their names:

The fact that a number of breweries in the USA are using the word Trappist to describe beers that are clearly not produced by Trappist monks has drawn the attention of the International Trappist Association, or I.T.A. The Association considers it a growing problem. For one thing, they don’t like the idea that a consumer might mistake a commercially brewed American beer for an Authentic Trappist brew.

Though I have some sympathy for their frustration, I think the bigger problem is that Americans don’t know what “Trappist” means. Â Forget getting confused about whether a beer is made by Trappist monks or not, before I started researching Belgian beer for my trip I didn’t know that there were monks making beer for public consumption, much less associate that with “Trappist”.

Cantillon

04/02/2012Beerbeer, belgium, brussels, lambic, tasting, tourescowles

This weekend, we visited Brasserie CantillonÂ in Brussels. This is a small, family-owned brewery that’s been in business since 1900 (and is still brewing beer pretty much the same way as when they started).

Cantillon is not far from the center of town, just a couple of blocks off a major boulevard near the main train station. Â The neighborhood is pretty nondescript, none of the shops were open at 10am on a Saturday. Â But once you open their big wooden door, it’s a completely different story. Â Right away you see the brewery is not like other breweries. Â It’s much more cozy, with a small tasting room and gift shop right as you enter, with aging bottles stacked all along the corridor leading back to the brewery proper.

The self-guided tour takes you through the brewery, where you can see their wood-clad mash tun, their beautiful copper kettles, their kegging and bottling lines, and fermentation tanks for the fruit beers. Â There is an endless array of barrels and bottles aging:

The real difference comes when you get to the attic, where you see their coolship — a giant shallow copper dish where the wort cools, and begins fermentation from wild yeast brought in on the wind.

Of course, no brewery tour is complete without a tasting. Â We tasted their gueuzeÂ and frambozen. Â We’d had their gueuze before and liked it quite a bit, but the frambozen was really fantastic. Â Unlike other frambozen we’d had before, it wasn’t overly sweet or cloying. Â Instead, it had more of an earthy, raspberry-leaf flavor that blended well with the lambic tartness.

Of the breweries I’ve seen, Cantillon has the most character, by far. Â Every other brewery I’ve seen, from a giant mega-brewery like Guinness to the tiny Swamp Head, is basically the same: a variety of stainless steel cylinders in a warehouse, connected by a crazy assortment of pipes. Â But Cantillon is completely different. Â Their equipment is from a completely different era, and is truly beautiful. Â The open fermentation runs completely counter to the sterilized and controlled fermentation standard everywhere else. Â They have some modern equipment too (including some modern cylindrical fermentation tanks for aging their fruit beers). Â But the core brewing operations are a living antique.

Quad Tasting Notes, Part 2

03/28/2012Beerbelgium, quad, tastingescowles

After our first round of quads, we wanted to try a few less popular versions, with mixed success:

  • Kasteel Donker: Neither Wind nor I cared for this at all — it has an overwhelming black cherry flavor, and not much else going on.
  • Malheur 12°: This was very nice, with a lot of spicy, almost peppery notes. Â Definitely taking the typical abbey complexity and spiciness to an extreme, and I think it works well.
  • Gulden Draak 9000: My favorite of the new brews, with a co2 bite and a boozy kick. Â Uncharacteristically medium amber color. Â Very smooth, clean flavor, in sharp contrast to the Malheur. Â As with the tripels, these two are a very good example in contrasting takes on the style that both work well.

We also had a couple of favorites (including another bottle of St. Bernardus to replace a spoiled bottle from the first round):

  • St. Bernardus Abt 12: An unspoiled bottle showed a wonderful complexity, with raisiny sweetness, spiciness, and earthiness.
  • Rochefort 10: A clear favorite of this batch for both of us, with a big boozy kick, but blended perfectly with sweet malt and spiciness.

Overall, I think I found the quads to be a little less varied than the tripels, but still differing quite a bit (and differing in the same way as the tripels — with some being very boozy but otherwise mild, and others being more spicy and earthy). Â The Kasteel Donker was the only beer in this group that I’d say was disappointing. Â I still haven’t had them side-by-side, so I’d be hard pressed to pick a favorite among the Westy 12, St. Bernardus 12 and Rochefort 10. Â I’d happily drink any of them any time.

Quad Tasting Notes, Part 1

03/21/2012Beerbelgium, netherlands, quad, tastingescowles

We’ve had quite enough tripel for now, so we decided to move on to quads. Â These are heavy enough that we’ll just do a few at a time. Â So we started with a few of our favorites:

  • La Trappe Quadrupel: The most commercial of the Trappist breweries (and the only one in the Netherlands). Â This quad was actually on sale at our local grocery store last week (no Guinness here, even for St. Paddy’s). Â It’s got a good CO2 bite up front, yielding to a nice malty sweetness. Â A little toffee. Â A very nice, well-rounded quad, with a lighter color than most.

  • Straffe Hendrik Quadrupel: This beer was first brewed by De Haalve Maan in the early 80’s for the inauguration of a statue of St. Arnold (patron saint of brewers) in Bruges. Â Sweet and creamy, with an herbal note that is almost like licorice (but not overpowering). Â Reminds me of some of the sweeter Russian Imperial Stouts I’ve tasted.

  • Westvleteren 12: Widely regarded as the best beer in the world. Â It’s got a ton of sweetness with malt, candi sugar, and some fig. Also spicy, earthy, and a definite boozy kick.   • St. Bernardus Abt 12: The former contract brewer of Westvleteren’s beers (before they brought them back in-house in ’92). We’ll definitely have to get another bottle for the next round.
