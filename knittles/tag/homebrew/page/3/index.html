Saison Experiment

06/03/2012Beer#9, beer, homebrew, saisonescowles

We have long wanted to brew our own saison. Â After all, it’s the style of beer that really opened my eyes to the possibilities of beer and got us more interested in brewing and sampling as much as we could. Â But it was only recently when I started reading about saison in the Oxford Companion to Beer that I really understood just how complicated it is.

First of all, while there is a general style of base beer that is the most popular for making saison, you can in fact make sour beer from any base beer. Â And then there are several different species of Brettanomyces yeast, each with its own character and intensity. Â There are also a couple different kinds of bacteria that sour beer. Â And of course, there are a bunch of variables in the souring process, such as when you add Brett./bacteria (primary fermentation, secondary, bottling), or how warm you ferment (Dupont is supposed to ferment at 90°, for example).

So, we found a Saison Dupont clone recipe online that looked about right, and picked up five different vials of yeast: a straight saison yeast, three different Brett. yeasts (B. bruxellensis, B. claussenii, and B. lambicus), and Belgian Sour Mix (with Brett. and bacteria).

This was our second all-grain brew, and it went pretty smoothly. Â Our 5-gallon water cooler mash tun didn’t hold temperature, but we kept adding hot water until it was warm enough. Â The mash and boil went great, and after adding water to make up five gallons, the gravity was exactly on target (1.057). Â We also used fine mesh hop sacks this time instead of cheesecloth, which made for much less gunk to filter out of the wort.

After the boil, we put the wort into our bottling bucket and filled five 1-gallon growlers, one with the Belgian Sour Mix, the second with just regular saison yeast, and the last three with saison yeast and one kind of Brett. Â We’re hoping this will help us figure out what kind of saison we want to brew a larger batch of later this summer. Â And if everything works out, this should be a great way to try out a bunch of different options in small batches. Â There are so many options, especially for mead and cider, and it would be nice to have a good process to try out a lot of options and see what we like.

Getting the beer in the keg was much easier than bottles.Â  It was easy to sterilize and siphon the beer into the keg.Â  But that’s where the smooth sailing stopped.Â  First, I found that I’d gotten ball-lock couplers and a pin-lock keg.Â  So back to the homebrew shop I went and picked up the right couplers.Â  Then I found that my store-bought 5/16″ gas line wouldn’t fit the 1/4″ pin-lock gas coupler.Â  I had never imagined that they might use different gas line sizes (I’ve been fiddling around with kegs and plumbing long enough to know better, but for some reason I still expect things to work out by default).Â  By the time I figured this out, the homebrew shop was closed and the Thanksgiving holiday was upon us, so the beer sat refrigerated but unpressurized over the long weekend.

Once back in town, the homebrew shop was still closed, but luckily my local hardware store helped me jury-rig an adapter.Â  So I hooked it all up and pressurized to my normal serving pressure (8-10 psi), but the keg was leaking CO2.Â  I tried reseating the lid and greasing the O-ring, with no success.Â  I’ve also read that increasing the pressure will help the seal (and speed up the carbonation process), so I cranked up the pressure to 25 psi.Â  As I increased the pressure, the sound of the CO2 leak changed in pitch, heading from a benign hiss to a worrying whine and then, just before making me truly worried, changed into a satisfying gurgle as the seal took hold and the keg started to pressurize properly.
